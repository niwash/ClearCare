# How to Read a HIQA Report

*A straightforward guide to nursing home inspection reports*

## 1. Start with the basic information

Find the nursing home or centre name, inspection date, report type and the areas covered. The date matters: a report records what inspectors found during that inspection, not necessarily the centre’s situation today. Check whether it is a standard inspection or a thematic inspection, because the layout and focus can differ.

## 2. Understand the judgements

HIQA uses judgement labels to show how the service met the relevant requirement at the time of inspection. Common labels include:

- **Compliant** — The requirement was met.
- **Substantially Compliant** — The requirement was largely met, but some improvement was needed.
- **Not Compliant** — The requirement was not met and action was needed.

Always read the exact wording and context in the report. Do not treat one label as a complete summary of every aspect of a home’s care.

## 3. Read the evidence, not just the label

The findings section explains why inspectors reached a judgement. Look for specific observations, records reviewed, risks identified, and whether an issue was corrected during the inspection. For example, a report may describe fire doors or evacuation plans, maintenance problems, infection-control concerns, care-plan gaps, incident notifications, staffing or training, or residents’ rights and activities. These are examples found in the 16 reports reviewed for ClearCare; they should not be assumed to occur in every nursing home.

## 4. Check for repeat issues and follow-up

If earlier reports are available, compare the same centre and relevant regulation or topic across inspection dates. Ask whether an issue has appeared before, whether the report describes improvement, and whether concerns remain. A single report may not show the full history, so avoid drawing conclusions without checking the available context.

## 5. Separate inspector findings from the provider’s response

A provider may set out actions it intends to take in a compliance plan. Keep that response distinct from the inspector’s judgement: a promise or planned action is not proof that the issue was later resolved. Look for evidence in a subsequent report where available.

## 6. Use the report to ask informed questions

Examples of useful follow-up questions include:

- What has changed since this inspection, and how can that be verified?
- Have previously identified concerns been addressed?
- How are care plans, safety checks and staff training reviewed?
- How can residents raise complaints or access meaningful activities?

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Confirm the centre, date and inspection type.
- [ ] Read each judgement with the supporting evidence.
- [ ] Note the regulation or topic and the source page.
- [ ] Look for repeat findings and check later reports.
- [ ] Keep provider plans separate from inspector conclusions.
- [ ] Use the report to prepare questions—not to create a simple ranking of homes.

## Final takeaway

Read the inspection date, judgement and supporting evidence together. Use the report to understand documented findings and prepare informed questions, rather than relying on a single label to make a decision.
